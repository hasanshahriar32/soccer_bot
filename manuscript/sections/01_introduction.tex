\section{Introduction}
Moving a suspect object away from people requires locating it, navigating toward it, positioning a gripper, transporting it and confirming release. Components can function in isolation while the complete task remains unreliable: detections become stale, gateways discard command magnitude, and navigation goals may lie outside the arm's contact workspace. The central engineering problem is the relationship between subsystem evidence and permission to act.

Mobile robots support nuclear decommissioning~\cite{bird2022vega}, while compact manipulators integrate mobility and reach~\cite{kemp2022stretch}. HazBot studies an accessible embedded implementation with four Pi-centered subsystems: navigation, wheel control, native visual inference and manipulation. Dedicated controllers separate sensor acquisition and actuator signaling from mission decisions.

The application is retrieval of designated objects from a potentially hazardous area. Visual recognition proposes a candidate; task authority determines whether it may be handled and where it should be deposited. Appearance alone does not establish chemical, radiological or biological properties. Ball-based simulated control supplies the nonhazardous surrogate context.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies a Pi-centered four-subsystem architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}) with explicit physical interfaces and separate drive, arm and ranging controller paths. Second, it defines a seven-phase reference coordinator with observable prerequisites for mobility--manipulation handoffs. Third, it provides reproducible model and source analyses that connect these prerequisites to wrist geometry and actual command semantics. The evaluation establishes properties of the discrete coordinator and source models, providing a traceable basis for subsequent physical retrieval experiments.
